# Research Workflow

This workflow converts a lead into a defensible public-interest finding.

## Stage 1 — Define the proposition

Write one precise question that can be tested. Avoid starting with a conclusion.

Example:

> Are ordinary North West water customers funding infrastructure required by large new commercial users such as data centres, or are those users paying their full incremental cost?

## Stage 2 — Decompose the question

Create testable sub-questions covering:

- what changed
- who decided it
- dates and legal authority
- beneficiaries
- direct and ultimate payers
- required infrastructure
- public/private risk allocation
- data collection and sharing
- safeguards and oversight
- relevant contracts, approvals, and regulators

## Stage 3 — Evidence register

For each source, record:

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Source | Organisation/document |
| Type | Primary / secondary |
| Publication date | Exact date |
| Period covered | Exact period |
| Claim supported | Specific proposition |
| Direct quote needed? | Only a short necessary extract |
| Limitations | Gaps/caveats |
| Reliability | Why it is credible |

Prefer original documents over summaries.

## Stage 4 — Timeline

Build a chronological table covering announcement, consultation, approval, procurement, contract, funding, implementation, revisions, delays, litigation/regulatory intervention, and cancellation where relevant.

A later press statement must not be allowed to overwrite what the earlier decision documents actually said.

## Stage 5 — Follow the money

Map every material flow that can be established:

`payer -> intermediary -> programme/project -> supplier/beneficiary`

Check:

- taxpayer funding
- customer bills
- borrowing
- developer contributions
- grants/subsidies
- guarantees
- tax relief
- dividends
- debt
- capital expenditure
- operating expenditure

For numerical comparisons, align definitions and periods before adding figures.

## Stage 6 — Follow the data

For digital/AI systems, map:

`data subject/source -> controller -> processor -> host -> administrator -> downstream recipient`

Establish where possible:

- ownership/control
- legal basis
- storage location
- query/access rights
- admin privileges
- subcontractors
- international transfers
- retention/deletion
- logs/auditability
- security controls
- contractual restrictions

Distinguish "the supplier says it cannot access data" from evidence establishing the technical, contractual, and legal controls that prevent access.

## Stage 7 — Compare narratives

Put these side by side:

1. public announcement
2. underlying policy/technical document
3. procurement/contract
4. regulator or audit finding
5. company statement
6. independent reporting/research

Mark contradictions and changes in wording.

## Stage 8 — Adversarial check

Search specifically for the strongest evidence against the working theory.

Ask:

- Is there a simpler explanation?
- Is the apparent connection only chronological?
- Are costs already recovered directly from the beneficiary?
- Did a later rule supersede the document being cited?
- Is a headline number gross rather than net?
- Is a supposed secret actually published elsewhere?
- Are legal restrictions stronger than the technical concern suggests?

A story that survives this stage is stronger.

## Stage 9 — Legal/status check

For named individuals or alleged wrongdoing, verify exact legal status immediately before publication. Distinguish allegations, investigations, charges, active proceedings, civil/tribunal findings, inquests, regulatory actions, and convictions.

Never infer guilt from political association, job title, investigation, or charge.

## Stage 10 — Confidence decision

Assign exactly one working label:

- CONFIRMED
- STRONGLY SUPPORTED
- PLAUSIBLE
- UNPROVEN
- FALSE OR MISLEADING

Write one sentence explaining why.

## Stage 11 — Publication test

Before publication, verify:

- headline does not overstate evidence
- every significant factual claim has a source
- dates and numbers were rechecked
- key counter-evidence is represented fairly
- fact and interpretation are visibly distinct
- allegations use legally accurate wording
- original documents are linked where possible
- unresolved gaps are stated rather than filled by assumption

## Stage 12 — Output structure

Recommended article/report structure:

1. Headline
2. Subhead
3. What happened and why it matters
4. What the underlying documents say
5. What changed
6. Follow the money
7. Follow the data, if relevant
8. What is missing/unanswered
9. Counter-evidence and limitations
10. Why it matters to the public
11. What we know / what the evidence suggests / what remains unproven
12. Primary sources
